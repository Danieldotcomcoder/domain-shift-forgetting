\section{Introduction}

Normalization layers such as LayerNorm \citep{ba2016layer} and RMSNorm \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm} are a standard
part of Transformer language models. In the common pre-norm arrangement \citep{xiong2020layer}, every attention
and MLP sub-layer reads its input through a normalizer that rescales each token's activation vector by that
token's own statistics. A long line of work has asked whether normalization is necessary at all
\citep{zhang2019fixup,brock2021nfnet,he2024simplifying,zhu2025dyt}. TaperNorm \citep{kanavalau2026tapernorm} is a
recent proposal for Transformers; we follow version~1 of its arXiv preprint throughout. It acts exactly as RMSNorm
(or LayerNorm) during an initial gate warm-up, calibrates a fixed scaling branch during that warm-up, and then
cosine-decays a global gate until each internal normalizer becomes a sample-independent linear map. That map can
be folded into the adjacent linear projections at inference. \citet{kanavalau2026tapernorm} report that this
matches normalized baselines under identical setups, and they measure up to $1.22\times$ higher throughput in an
inference microbenchmark.

This study looks at a property that the original evaluation did not target: behaviour under a later shift in the
training distribution. A normalizer is invariant to the scale of its input. If continued training on a new
domain changes activation magnitudes, an RMSNorm layer absorbs part of that change automatically, whereas a
tapered layer applies a scale calibrated on the earlier distribution. One can conjecture that the tapered network
must then absorb the shift through weight updates, which might overwrite structure that supports the original
domain and so increase catastrophic forgetting
\citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic,french1999catastrophic,kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. This argument motivates the
hypothesis; the experiment tests the hypothesis, not the mechanism.

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} \textbf{H1}: after training switches from web text to Python code, internal TaperNorm
(without the auxiliary scale loss; final RMSNorm retained) increases the persistent deterioration of held-out web
cross-entropy, relative to continuing web training, compared with internal RMSNorm. \emph{Persistent} is defined
in \cref{sec:estimands}.

We report two studies. \textbf{Study 1} is a deliberately small, inexpensive pilot that asked whether H1
deserves a larger study (\cref{sec:design,sec:execution,sec:results}). Its design pairs the two conditions on
initialization and data order and branches each condition's trained prefix into a web continuation and a Python
continuation from an identical saved state. It fixes a difference-in-differences endpoint and an ordered set of
decision rules before any training. Its pre-specified decision was to stop. A pilot with one domain pair and
three seeds says little about the hypothesis in general, and an exploratory analysis of the pilot suggested that
the premise of H1, a domain-induced change in activation scale, was barely present for Python. We therefore ran
\textbf{Study 2} (\cref{sec:study2}), a separate study whose protocol and code were frozen, and their hash submitted
for timestamping, before any of its jobs ran. It chose a new domain by a fixed rule and tested H1's direction there on six seeds
(\textbf{H2}). It also replicated Study 1's web-to-Python contrast on three fresh seeds (\textbf{R2}) and measured
the scale premise directly as a pre-specified manipulation check. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item a paired, pre-specified design for measuring how an architectural choice affects forgetting under a
        domain shift, whose primary contrast decomposes exactly into branch-level forgetting, a control-branch
        term, per-document effects and per-token-class effects;
  \item Study 1's result. All validity checks pass and the pre-specified decision is \emph{stop: small observed
        effect}. Mean $D$ = \Dmean{}~nats/token, against a $+0.015$ bound for a meaningful excess;
  \item Study 2's results. The selected domain is Chinese web text. There, mean $D_X$ over six seeds was
        \twoDmean{} (95\% interval $[\twoDlo, \twoDhi]$), which falls just past the pre-specified $-0.03$
        threshold into the \emph{opposite direction} category; that label depends on single seeds, but the
        pre-specified reading does not. On the fresh seeds the web-to-Python result replicated (\emph{stop}; mean
        $D$ = \repDmean). A pre-specified in-training measure of the premise, the domain-specific change in
        activation scale at the normalizer inputs, was \mcRatioLo--\mcRatioHi{} times larger for Chinese than for
        Python, yet TaperNorm did not forget more;
  \item a cautionary measurement of seed variation. Study 1's three paired seeds agreed closely (SD \Dsd), and
        three fresh seeds then gave web-to-Python contrasts of the opposite sign; the six seeds' SD is about
        \poolSdRatio{} times Study 1's;
  \item a lightweight procedure for fixing a cloud-executed study's plan before it runs without a prior registry
        entry, with an account of what it does and does not establish. The frozen protocol and code are hashed
        and the hash timestamped, every cloud notebook embeds the hash, and saved model states that are reused
        across sessions are re-evaluated bit-exactly against their own records before they train;
  \item code, frozen configurations, data hashes and all evaluation and training records, sufficient to recompute
        every number in this paper.
\end{itemize}
Both studies are small (\paramsRMS{} parameters, at most six seeds, two domain pairs) and should be read as
screening results, not as a general statement about TaperNorm (\cref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Normalization and its removal.} LayerNorm \citep{ba2016layer} and RMSNorm \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm}
normalize each token independently, and the pre-norm placement \citep{xiong2020layer} is now standard in
decoder-only language models. Fixup \citep{zhang2019fixup} and NFNets \citep{brock2021nfnet} train deep residual
networks without normalization through initialization, scaling and clipping choices. For Transformers,
\citet{he2024simplifying} study which standard components of the block can be removed, and
\citet{zhu2025dyt} replace normalization layers with an element-wise $\tanh$ map. TaperNorm
\citep{kanavalau2026tapernorm} instead removes internal normalization gradually during training. Its analysis
attributes the essential role of normalization to the \emph{output} normalizer, which anchors scale; this is why
our Taper-minus condition keeps the final RMSNorm.

\paragraph{Normalization, weight decay and effective learning rate.} When a normalizer makes a network's output
invariant to the scale of some weights, weight decay no longer regularizes those weights in the usual sense;
it shrinks them and thereby raises their effective learning rate \citep{vanlaarhoven2017l2,zhang2019weightdecay}.
\citet{dohare2024loss} document a loss of plasticity in deep continual learning, and
\citet{lyle2024normalization} connect normalization and effective learning rates to the loss of plasticity under
nonstationary training. These results bear on our exploratory scale analysis (\cref{sec:exploratory}).

\paragraph{Forgetting under continued training.} Catastrophic forgetting
\citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic,french1999catastrophic} is the loss of earlier competence when a network is
trained on new data, and it has motivated methods such as elastic weight consolidation
\citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. The training regime, including learning-rate decay, affects how much a network
forgets \citep{mirzadeh2020training}; larger pre-trained models forget less \citep{ramasesh2022scale}; and the
choice of normalization layer matters in online continual learning \citep{pham2022continual}. In language
modelling, continued pre-training on a new domain improves in-domain performance \citep{gururangan2020dont}, but
it can degrade performance on the original distribution; \citet{gupta2023rewarm} and \citet{ibrahim2024simple}
study how learning-rate re-warming, re-decaying and replay trade adaptation against this forgetting. Our design
deliberately uses no replay, re-warming or other mitigation. It isolates how much forgetting the normalization
choice itself causes, relative to a matched control branch.

\paragraph{Pre-specification.} Fixing hypotheses, endpoints and analysis rules before seeing outcomes protects
against flexible analysis \citep{nosek2018preregistration}. Neither study was lodged with a registry before it ran.
\Cref{sec:prespec,sec:s2prespec} state exactly what was fixed, and when, and what evidence supports each claim.
For Study 2 that evidence includes a hash of the frozen protocol and code, timestamped through OpenTimestamps
\citep{todd2016opentimestamps}, which anchors digests in the Bitcoin blockchain without revealing the document.

\section{Study 1: design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Notation and estimands}
\label{sec:estimands}

We write $u$ for the global optimizer update ($u = 1,\dots,15{,}260$) and $s = u - 9{,}156$ for the continuation
update within a branch. Two different events fall on the number 6{,}104: the TaperNorm gate reaches zero at global
update $u = 6{,}104$, and the primary endpoint is continuation update $s = 6{,}104$ (global update $u = 15{,}260$).
They coincide because the protocol ends the taper at 40\% of the 15{,}260-update trajectory, which equals the
continuation length. We always state whether a number is a $u$ or an $s$.

Let $L(m, b, s)$ be the token-weighted cross-entropy on the held-out web development set (nats per supervised
token, 2{,}097{,}152 labels), for normalization condition $m \in \{\mathrm{R}, \mathrm{T}\}$ (RMS, Taper-minus),
after $s$ continuation updates on branch $b \in \{\text{web}, \text{code}\}$. Let $L(m,\text{prefix})$ be the
same quantity at the switch. The primary endpoint is
\begin{equation}
  D(s) = \big[L(\mathrm{T},\text{code},s) - L(\mathrm{T},\text{web},s)\big] - \big[L(\mathrm{R},\text{code},s) - L(\mathrm{R},\text{web},s)\big],
  \label{eq:d}
\end{equation}
evaluated at $s = 6{,}104$. Positive $D$ means that, relative to its own web-continuation control, the Taper-minus
model loses more web performance under the Python shift than the RMS model does. Within each condition the
code-minus-web difference cancels everything the two branches share, including the condition-specific quality of
the prefix and generic continued-training drift.

\paragraph{Persistent.} We call a deterioration \emph{persistent} if it is present at the fixed endpoint
$s = 6{,}104$, after 100M continuation tokens and at the end of the learning-rate schedule. A difference visible
only early in continuation ($s \le 1{,}000$) is \emph{transient}, which the decision rules treat separately
(\cref{tab:rules}, rule~5).

We also report the actual forgetting $F(m,b) = L(m,b,6104) - L(m,\text{prefix})$ and the change in the
between-condition gap during web continuation,
$G_{\text{web}} = [L(\mathrm{T},\text{prefix}) - L(\mathrm{R},\text{prefix})] -
[L(\mathrm{T},\text{web},6104) - L(\mathrm{R},\text{web},6104)]$. These satisfy the identity
\begin{equation}
  D = \underbrace{F(\mathrm{T},\text{code}) - F(\mathrm{R},\text{code})}_{Q} + G_{\text{web}},
\end{equation}
so $Q = D - G_{\text{web}}$ is the direct difference in Python-branch forgetting. The identity is checked
numerically in the analysis code. Because CE is token-weighted, $D$ decomposes exactly into per-document terms
$D_i$ (weighted by each document's scored-label count) and into four disjoint GPT-2 token classes: whitespace
(W), text containing an alphanumeric character (A), other punctuation and symbols (P), and special, control or
invalid-fragment tokens (C). A fifth, overlapping flag R marks rare tokens: those that occur fewer than 100 times
in that seed's prefix training stream.

\subsection{Models and conditions}

All models are pre-norm decoder-only Transformers adapted from nanoGPT \citep{karpathy2022nanogpt}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item 6 layers, width 256, 4 heads of size 64, and an exact-GELU MLP of width 1{,}024;
  \item learned absolute positions, context 512, no biases and no dropout;
  \item GPT-2 token embeddings \citep{radford2019gpt2}, tied to the output layer (vocabulary 50{,}257).
\end{itemize}
The RMS model has \paramsRMS{} parameters; Taper-minus adds 3{,}072 for its second gain vectors. About
\embedShare\% of all parameters are in the token-embedding table. Both conditions keep the final RMSNorm.

\paragraph{Initialization.} All linear and embedding weights (token and position) are drawn from
$\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$. The two residual output projections of each block (the attention output projection and
the MLP down-projection) use $\mathcal{N}\big(0, (0.02/\sqrt{12})^2\big)$, where $12 = 2 \times 6$ layers. All gains
start at~1.

\paragraph{RMS.} All 12 internal normalizers are RMSNorm with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$.

\paragraph{Taper-minus.} All 12 internal normalizers are TaperNorm, following the operator and calibration of
\citet{kanavalau2026tapernorm} (their Section~3 and Appendix~C), without their auxiliary scale loss. For an input
$h \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at global update $u$, with gains $\gamma$ and $\tilde\gamma$,
\begin{equation}
  r(h) = \sqrt{\lVert h\rVert_2^2/d + 10^{-6}}, \qquad
  y = g(u)\, \frac{h}{r(h)} \odot \gamma + \big(1 - g(u)\big)\, c\, h \odot \tilde\gamma .
\end{equation}
The scalar $c$ of each site is calibrated during updates $u = 1$--$763$ as the ratio of bias-corrected
exponential moving averages (one EMA step per optimizer update, new-sample weight $0.01$) of
$\lVert h\odot\gamma\rVert^2 / r(h)$ and $\lVert h\odot\gamma\rVert^2$. These are collected from the pre-step
forward passes of every microbatch. After update $u = 763$, $c$ is frozen and $\tilde\gamma$ is initialized from
$\gamma$; $\tilde\gamma$ first receives an optimizer step at $u = 764$ and stays trainable. The gate is $g(u) = 1$
for $u \le 763$, $g(u) = \tfrac12[1 + \cos(\pi (u - 763)/5341)]$ for $763 < u < 6104$, and $g(u) = 0$ for
$u \ge 6104$. At $g = 0$ no per-token statistic is computed and $\gamma$ receives no gradient. Until $u = 763$ the
two conditions are mathematically identical. The gate timing, GELU, learned positions, weight decay,
learning-rate schedule and domain branching are choices of this study, so this is an adaptation of TaperNorm, not
a replication of its experiments.

\paragraph{Optimization.} The optimizer is AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with $\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$ and
$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. All two-dimensional parameters, embeddings included, get weight decay $0.1$; gain vectors
get none. Gradients are clipped once per update to global norm $1.0$, after accumulation. Each update uses
$32 \times 512 = 16{,}384$ supervised tokens. The learning rate rises linearly to $6\times10^{-4}$ over the first
305 updates and then follows a cosine decay to $6\times10^{-5}$ at $u = 15{,}260$. The branches continue this
global clock, with no learning-rate restart, optimizer reset, replay or regularization toward the prefix
(\cref{fig:design}).

\paragraph{Pairing.} Seeds 101, 102 and 103 are paired across conditions. A canonical RMS initialization is
generated from the seed and explicitly copied into the Taper-minus model; $\tilde\gamma$ starts at~1 and is
inactive until $u = 764$. Both conditions read the same pre-generated window order. Each run consists of a prefix
of 9{,}156 updates (150{,}011{,}904 tokens) followed by two branches of 6{,}104 updates each (100{,}007{,}936
tokens). The web branch reads further unseen web windows and the Python branch reads Python windows; no window
repeats within a trajectory. The primary allocation is 2.10B supervised tokens.

\paragraph{Precision.} Training ran in FP16 autocast with dynamic loss scaling \citep{micikevicius2018mixed}.
Master weights, optimizer moments, normalization and calibration statistics, and the loss were kept in FP32.
A step whose gradients were nonfinite was to be retried on the same batch at a halved loss scale, so no update
is ever skipped. This replaced the protocol's original BF16 plan, because the available hardware (NVIDIA T4)
lacks efficient BF16 support (\cref{tab:amendments}).

\subsection{Data}

\paragraph{Sources and splits.} Web text comes from C4 English \citep{raffel2020t5}. Training uses the official
training split, and the official validation split is hashed 50/50 into development and reserved-test sets. Code
comes from the Python subset of The Stack (deduplicated) \citep{kocetkov2022stack}. The dataset revisions are
\texttt{\cfourRev} (C4) and \texttt{\stackRev} (The Stack). Python files are grouped into connected components
over their repository-name aliases (\repoGroups{} groups). Each group is assigned to a split by the value of
$\mathrm{sha256}(\text{group}) \bmod 100$: 0--89 train, 90--94 development and 95--99 test. Empty documents, and
Python files without a usable repository name, were dropped.

\paragraph{Deduplication.} Documents are deduplicated exactly by content hash across all domains and splits. An
approximate near-duplicate search uses MinHash \citep{broder1997resemblance} over GPT-2 token 5-grams (128
permutations, LSH with 32 bands of 4 rows), confirmed by exact Jaccard similarity $\ge 0.85$. When duplicates
straddle splits, the copy in the higher-priority split is kept (test over development over training). This
removed \exactRemoved{} exact and \nearRemoved{} near duplicates. Documents shorter than five tokens have no
5-gram and are covered only by exact deduplication (\shortDocs{} documents).

\paragraph{Recall of the near-duplicate search.} With 32 bands of 4 rows, a pair with Jaccard similarity $J$
becomes an LSH candidate with probability $1 - (1 - J^4)^{32}$. A pair at $J = 0.85$ is therefore missed with
probability \lshMiss, and a pair at $J = 0.70$ is still found with probability \lshRecallSeventy. This analytic
recall is the evidence that near duplicates above the threshold were found. The preparation code also compared
a deterministic random sample of \auditPairs{} cross-split document pairs by exact Jaccard, and none reached 0.85.
Because two randomly chosen documents are almost never near duplicates, that comparison is a sanity check with
negligible power to detect leakage, not evidence of its absence.

\paragraph{Packing and quotas.} Text is tokenized with GPT-2 BPE, with one EOS appended per document, and packed
into 513-token windows at stride 512 with no padding. Windows are packed across document boundaries without an
attention mask: a window can hold the end of one document, an EOS token and the start of the next, and attention
can cross the EOS. Positions restart at 0 in every window. Each label is attributed to its own document (a label
after an EOS belongs to the new document), so per-document sums reconstruct the corpus CE exactly. Quotas are
\webTrainTokens{} web and \pyTrainTokens{} Python training tokens, and 2{,}097{,}152 development labels (4{,}096
windows) per domain. The \emph{quick} development set is the first 262{,}144 of those labels. Reserved-test sets
of 8{,}388{,}608 labels per domain were sealed and never used. The non-whitespace code CE used to measure
adaptation excludes class W only.

\subsection{Evaluation schedule and decision rules}

Full development evaluations ran at 13 prefix points and at 25 continuation points per branch. Quick development
evaluations ran at the switch and at 80 continuation points per branch, dense early on (\cref{app:schedule}).
Every full evaluation stores per-document and per-class sufficient statistics.

The secondary \emph{matched-adaptation} analysis is fixed in advance. It fixes targets at the RMS Python branch's
quick-dev code CE at $s \in \{1525, 3050, 6104\}$. It finds the first downward crossing of each target by
Taper-minus within an observed bracket of at most 100 updates (no extrapolation) and interpolates Taper-minus's
web CE at that crossing. It then compares the two models' web forgetting relative to their own $s = 0$.

The decision rules in \cref{tab:rules} are applied in order once all three seed groups are complete. They are
screening criteria, not significance tests. With three seeds, the reported 95\% interval
$\bar D \pm 4.303\, s_D/\sqrt{3}$ is descriptive.

\paragraph{Choice of thresholds.} The protocol fixed the thresholds of 0.015 and 0.03~nats/token in advance as
investment thresholds for a screening pilot. It records no power analysis or formal derivation for them, and we
do not supply one after the fact. For orientation, a cross-entropy difference of 0.015 or 0.03~nats/token
corresponds to a 1.5\% or 3.0\% change in perplexity ($e^{0.015} \approx 1.015$, $e^{0.03} \approx 1.030$).
A mean between the two thresholds falls into the inconclusive category rather than into either decision.

\subsection{Pre-specification, timeline and amendments}
\label{sec:prespec}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The hypothesis, design, estimands, evaluation schedule and decision thresholds come from a
written protocol (``protocol v3''), drafted on 12 September 2026, before the corpus was prepared or any model was
trained on it. The protocol text is included in the project repository. It was not lodged with an external
registry, which is a limitation (\cref{sec:limitations}). The amendments in \cref{tab:amendments} were made before
any primary-run update; none of them changes the model, data, token budgets, seeds, endpoint or thresholds.

\paragraph{Timeline of the primary run (3 October 2026, UTC).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \sessionStartTime: the run starts.
  \item \logConfigTime: the run log records the frozen configuration hash (\texttt{\configSha\ldots}). The
        hashed configuration contains the decision thresholds, and the hash can be recomputed from the released
        \texttt{config.json} by the runner's own procedure.\footnote{The hash is \emph{not} the SHA-256 of the
        file itself; \texttt{sha256sum config.json} gives a different value. To reproduce it, load the JSON, remove
        the keys \texttt{config\_sha256} and \texttt{checkpoint\_seconds}, serialize the rest as canonical JSON
        (keys sorted, separators \texttt{","} and \texttt{":"}, no whitespace) and take the SHA-256 of the UTF-8
        bytes. \texttt{paper/README.md} gives a one-line command, and \texttt{paper/make\_assets.py} asserts the
        match on every build.}
  \item \seedOneFirst--\seedOneLast: seed 101's four endpoint cross-entropies (both conditions, both branches)
        appear in the run's live log. Seed 101's $D$ could have been computed from them.
  \item \commitTime: commit \texttt{3ee1dc4} is made, containing the protocol text and the runner script.
  \item \sessionEndTime: the run ends, and the same process applies the decision rules and writes the decision
        report.
\end{itemize}
Because the commit came after seed 101's endpoint values had been printed, the commit on its own does not
establish pre-specification, and we disclose that here and in \cref{sec:limitations}. The evidence that the
analysis was fixed in advance is different. The thresholds used by the run were bound into the configuration
hash logged at \logConfigTime, before any outcome existed. The runner script, whose decision thresholds appear on

\section{Study 1: execution and implementation checks}
\label{sec:execution}

Study 1's primary run took one \sessionHours-hour session on a cloud machine with two NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs (PyTorch
\torchVersion, CUDA \cudaVersion), with RMS on one GPU and Taper-minus on the other. All six condition--seed runs
completed all 21{,}364 updates. There were no nonfinite losses, gradients or weights; the loss scale grew to
\maxLossScale{} and never needed a retry (\overflowRetries{} retries in total). GPU kernels used for the backward
pass are nondeterministic, so results are reproducible to within numerical noise, not bitwise.

conditions; we report how often it was active, per phase, from the per-update training logs (\cref{tab:clipping}).

reached zero it became more frequent, more so for Taper-minus (\clipGateZeroTaper\% vs \clipGateZeroRMS\% of
the remaining prefix updates). On both continuation branches it was active in \clipBranchLo--\clipBranchHi\%
of updates in both conditions. We report these rates descriptively and did not analyse their consequences.

\paragraph{Implementation checks.} The training code is a single script that transcribes a separately tested

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=1pt]

\subsection{Exploratory analyses (not pre-specified)}
\label{sec:exploratory}

The analyses in this subsection were chosen after the results were known. They are descriptive, and none of
them enters the decision.

\paragraph{Activation scales at the normalizer inputs.} The run logged forward-only probes of the activation
$h$ entering each of the 12 internal normalizers, on the last 256 training windows of each domain (131{,}072
positions per domain), at the switch and at the end of each branch. For each site we take the scale to be the
root mean square of $\lVert h\rVert$ over the probe positions and work with natural-log ratios of scales, which
treat growth and shrinkage symmetrically. For the absolute quantities ($|\cdot|$) we take the absolute log-ratio
for each of the \scalePairs{} site--seed pairs and then average; the signed quantities are plain averages, also
shown as multiplicative factors. \Cref{tab:scales} gives three quantities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The code-vs-web gap at the switch.} The mean absolute log-ratio was \scaleGapR{} (RMS) and
        \scaleGapT{} (Taper-minus): code and web activations differed in scale by roughly \scaleGapPctR\% and
        \scaleGapPctT\%.
  \item \textbf{The change in the web-probe scale during each branch.} In the RMS model the scale shrank, by a
        factor of \scaleWebFactorR{} in the web branch (\scaleWebShrinkR{} of \scalePairs{} site--seed pairs
        shrank) and \scalePyFactorR{} in the Python branch (\scalePyShrinkR{} of \scalePairs). In Taper-minus it
        changed much less and mostly grew, by factors of \scaleWebFactorT{} and \scalePyFactorT{} (mean absolute
        log-ratios \scaleWebAbsT{} and \scalePyAbsT).
  \item \textbf{The code-specific part of that change} (Python branch minus web branch, per site and seed): a
        mean absolute log-ratio of \scaleSpecificR{} (RMS) and \scaleSpecificT{} (Taper-minus), about
        \scaleSpecificPctR\% in both.
\end{itemize}
The largest scale change, the shrinkage in the RMS model, happened in both branches, so it was not caused by the
code data. The code-specific change was small and the same size in both architectures. Study 2 turned these
measures into a pre-specified manipulation check (\cref{sec:s2results}).

\paragraph{Document bootstrap.} To see how much of the uncertainty in $D$ comes from the finite development set,
we resampled the \nDocsWeb{} web development documents with replacement (\bootB{} replicates; the same resampled
documents for all arms and seeds) and recomputed the endpoint $D$. The 95\% percentile interval for mean $D$ is
$[\bootLo, \bootHi]$, with a bootstrap standard error of \bootSE; per-seed standard errors are
\bootSeedSElo--\bootSeedSEhi{} (\cref{app:extra}). This interval treats the trained models as fixed, so it
measures only evaluation-sample noise. Comparing like with like, the between-seed SD of $D$ (\Dsd) is about
\bootRatioSeed{} times the per-seed bootstrap standard error, and the seed-based standard error of the mean
(\seedSEmean) is about \bootRatioMean{} times the bootstrap standard error of the mean. Uncertainty about $D$ is
therefore dominated by training variation across seeds, and the bootstrap interval must not be read as
uncertainty about the effect itself.

\section{Study 2: a second domain, a replication and a manipulation check}
\label{sec:study2}

Study 1 left two questions open. Its exploratory scale analysis suggested that the premise of H1 was weak for
Python, so its null result says little about a shift that does move activation scales; and three seeds estimate
seed-to-seed variation poorly. Study 2 is a separate study with its own protocol.\footnote{The frozen protocol
calls itself ``pre-registered'' (for example, in the heading of its Section~9.4) and calls R2 ``confirmatory''.
That wording was written before Amendment~1 deferred the public registry entry, and the frozen text cannot be
changed. In this paper Study 2 is described as specified in advance and hash-timestamped
(\cref{sec:s2prespec}), and R2 as a replication within a screening study.} It adds no seeds to Study 1's
analysis and changes none of Study 1's endpoints or decisions. It asks three questions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{H2 (primary).} After a switch from web text to a new domain $X$, chosen by a fixed rule, does
        Taper-minus show more persistent forgetting of held-out web text than RMS, relative to continuing web
        training? The estimand $D_X$ is \cref{eq:d} with the $X$ branch in place of the Python branch, at
        $s = 6{,}104$, over seeds 101--106. As before, positive values mean that Taper-minus forgets more.
  \item \textbf{R2 (replication).} Does Study 1's web-to-Python result replicate on three fresh seeds (104--106),
        with Study 1's estimand, guardrails and decision rules unchanged?
  \item \textbf{M (manipulation check, descriptive).} Was the scale premise in fact stronger for $X$ than for
        Python? M applies the measures of \cref{sec:exploratory}, now pre-specified, to both domains in the same
        models.
\end{itemize}
Following the protocol, we write $X$ for the selected domain. To avoid a clash of names, Study 1's token class of
special, control and invalid-fragment tokens is called C throughout this paper.

\subsection{Selecting the domain}
\label{sec:s2selection}

The protocol fixed four candidates, each pinned to a dataset revision: German, Russian and Chinese web text from
the multilingual C4 corpus (mC4) \citep{xue2021mt5}, at the same revision as Study 1's C4, and mathematical web
text from OpenWebMath \citep{paster2024openwebmath}. Other programming languages were not candidates, since
Study 1 had found the premise weak for code. For each candidate, a probe sample of 256 windows from its training
side (131{,}072 positions, the size of Study 1's probes) was passed forward through Study 1's six switch states.
The selection statistic is
\begin{equation}
  S(c) = \operatorname{mean}\, \Big| \tfrac12 \ln \big( E_c / E_{\text{web}} \big) \Big| ,
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the mean squared norm of the activation entering a normalizer and the mean runs over 12 sites, three
seeds and both conditions. This is the switch-time gap of \cref{sec:exploratory}. The candidate with the largest
$S$ becomes $X$. Before selecting, the probe had to reproduce Study 1's recorded switch-time energies for web and
Python within a relative $10^{-3}$; the reproduction was exact (largest relative difference \twoReproMax).

\paragraph{Data.} The Chinese data were prepared with Study 1's pipeline (GPT-2 tokenization, packing, and exact
and MinHash near-duplicate removal), with two additions. Documents that exactly duplicated any of Study 1's
documents, or nearly duplicated one of its development documents, were removed; and the audit also compared

regenerated bit for bit before the new orders were written.

GPT-2's byte-level BPE \citep{radford2019gpt2} was trained mostly on English, and it splits most Chinese
characters into byte fragments that are not valid UTF-8 on their own. \twoCtrlShare\% of the Chinese development
labels therefore fall in class C, against \webCtrlShare\% for web text. The Chinese shift is as much a shift in
which tokens the model must predict as a shift in content.

\subsection{Pre-specification and timeline}
\label{sec:s2prespec}

The protocol was written after Study 1's results and paper were known; its Section~12.2 lists everything that was
known or inspected beforehand. No activation of any candidate domain had been measured, and no Study 2 job had
run. The author then decided to execute Study 2 before filing a public registry entry. Amendment~1, made before any
job, records that decision and adds the measures below. A registration manifest
(\texttt{study2/registration-manifest.json}, SHA-256 \texttt{\twoManifestSha\ldots}) lists the SHA-256 of the
protocol, including the amendment, and of every Study 2 code file. All times are UTC.\footnote{The protocol's own
dates (8 and 9 October 2026) are the author's local dates, UTC+3.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \twoSubmit: the manifest's SHA-256 is submitted to four OpenTimestamps calendar servers. Only the hash
        leaves the author's computer.
  \item \twoFirstJob: the first Study 2 job, the domain probe, is created on Kaggle. Every Study 2 notebook embeds
        the manifest byte for byte, and Kaggle records each version's creation time on its own servers. The probe,
        the data preparation and every training session also write the manifest's SHA-256 into their outputs.
  \item \twoRunStart: the main run starts.
  \item \twoBlockTime: Bitcoin block \twoBlock{} is mined, carrying the timestamp attestation of the manifest.
\end{itemize}

The Bitcoin attestation is the only one of these records that depends on neither the author nor a single service
provider, but it is also the weakest bound. Block times are accurate only to about two hours, and by the time of
this block the $X$ branches of seeds 101--103 had already reached their endpoints inside the running session. The
time of submission to the calendars comes from the author's machine; the calendar operators record it, but the
proof does not establish it. That the plan was fixed before any outcome existed therefore rests on Kaggle's
server-side version times: the notebook versions that embed the manifest were created between \twoFirstJob{} and
the start of the main run. (The times given here were printed by the author's machine at each push; Kaggle's
version history records them independently.) Three further facts limit what could have changed after the run
started. The decisions were computed in-process by the runner, its decision thresholds are constants in its
source, and every session recorded a runner SHA-256 equal to the registered one. Amendment~1 defers the public
registry entry until after execution, so that entry cannot establish pre-specification on its own. Study 2
should be read as \emph{specified in advance and hash-timestamped before any run, with public registration
deferred until after execution}, not as conventionally pre-registered.

\subsection{Execution and implementation checks}
\label{sec:s2execution}

Before the main run, a smoke test exercised the whole protocol end to end on toy models (with interruptions and
resumes), checked the lineage of Study 1's six switch states, and measured throughput on seed 104's prefix only;
it produced no outcome data. The main run then used two sessions on two T4 GPUs, of \twoSessOne{} and \twoSessTwo{}
hours (\twoSessTotal{} hours in all), with PyTorch \twoTorch{} in the Kaggle image of Study 1's run.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item All 12 condition--seed runs completed. There were no restarts, missing records, failures or integrity
        problems, and there were \twoOverflow{} FP16 overflow retries.
  \item All \twoLineageN{} lineage checks were bit-exact: every restored switch state re-evaluated to exactly its
        recorded cross-entropies and energies, including Study 1's six switch states in a new session.
  \item Training took \twoSecRMS{}~s per update in each condition. Gradient clipping was active in \twoClipXR\%
        (RMS) and \twoClipXT\% (Taper-minus) of Chinese-branch updates, against about \twoClipWebR\% and
        \twoClipPyR\% on the web and Python branches of the RMS models of the fresh seeds (\cref{app:s2extra}).
  \item The decision report written by the runner was recomputed independently from the downloaded records, and
        every contrast, interval and manipulation-check value agrees with it (\texttt{paper/make\_assets.py}
        asserts this on every build).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pre-specified reading.} H2's category is opposite direction and the premise was stronger for $X$ in
training, so the matrix (\cref{tab:s2matrix}) permits the reading \emph{evidence against the scale-mismatch account
of H1 in this setting}. Without the equivalence reading, its second sentence does not apply. The reading is
sturdier than the label. Every leave-one-out mean stays below $+0.015$, so dropping any one seed would leave the
category in the same row of the matrix, which groups stop, opposite and transient, and the permitted reading
would be unchanged; only the label \emph{opposite direction} depends on single seeds. The reading inherits the
limits of the manipulation check, which is measured after treatment. Because of the qualifications above, we read
the opposite-direction category as no evidence for H1 on Chinese, and not as evidence that TaperNorm protects
against forgetting.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the two studies show.} Over two domain shifts and twelve seed-level contrasts, replacing internal
RMSNorm with TaperNorm did not produce the excess forgetting that H1 predicts. Neither shift reached the
pre-specified screen for a meaningful excess. For Python, Study 1's mean was negative and the fresh seeds' mean,
although positive, stayed below 0.015. For Chinese, the mean was negative and fell just into the opposite-direction
category. How firmly an excess of $+0.015$ is ruled out differs, by the post hoc one-sided reading of
\cref{sec:s2results}: firmly for Chinese, for Python only when the six seeds are pooled, and not by the three fresh
Python seeds alone. Study 2 also measured the premise with a pre-specified in-training check. Beyond what
continued web training did, the Chinese shift changed activation scales during training about four times as much
as Python did, and the two architectures responded differently: the RMS models' scale shrank and the Taper-minus
models' grew. Taper-minus nevertheless did not forget more. Under Study 2's pre-specified interpretation matrix
this is evidence against the scale-mismatch account of H1 in this setting, subject to the limits of a check
measured after treatment.

\paragraph{What they do not show.} \emph{Stop} is not evidence of equivalence \citep{lakens2017equivalence}, and
neither study met its equivalence reading. Nor is the opposite-direction category good evidence that TaperNorm
protects against forgetting. The Chinese mean lay just past the threshold, its 95\% interval includes zero,
leaving out either of two single seeds would change the label to \emph{stop}, and a single seed's endpoint is
noisy. The Chinese shift was also nearly
catastrophic for web text in both architectures (\cref{fig:s2traj}a), a regime in which the difference between
them is a small fraction of the total forgetting. In Study 1, apart from a brief early dip, the negative values of
$D$ appeared only in the final, low-learning-rate phase, and the training regime, including learning-rate decay,
is known to affect forgetting \citep{mirzadeh2020training}. All intervals are conditional on one prepared corpus
sample per domain and one training recipe; they are not population bounds.

\paragraph{Seed variation.} The most consequential lesson for a design like ours concerns variance. Study 1's
three seeds agreed closely (SD \Dsd), which made its descriptive interval narrow, and Study 2's protocol used that
SD for planning. The fresh seeds then gave web-to-Python contrasts of the opposite sign, outside Study 1's
interval, and the Chinese contrasts varied about ten times as much as Study 1's had. Seed-to-seed variation in
neural-network training can be large \citep{dodge2020finetuning}, and three seeds can understate it badly.
Pairing on initialization and data order removes variation that the two conditions share, but not the variation
that builds up differently in each of them over 15{,}260 updates. Future designs of this kind need many more seeds
than we used, or an endpoint that averages several late evaluations rather than relying on one.

\paragraph{The premise and the mechanism.} Study 1's exploratory analysis found the scale premise weak for Python:
code and web activations differed in scale by only about \scaleGapPctR--\scaleGapPctT\% at the switch, and the
largest change during continuation, a shrinkage in the RMS model, happened to a similar extent when training simply
continued on web text. Study 2's in-training check found a premise about four times stronger for Chinese and
still no excess forgetting, which makes it less likely that H1 failed only because the premise was absent. That
check, however, is measured after treatment and cannot separate cause from symptom: a larger domain-specific scale
change may partly reflect the heavier forgetting of the Chinese branch, and the measure taken before treatment,
the switch-time gap, was not stronger for Chinese. We tested no mechanism, but several features of the setting may
matter:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Taper-minus keeps the final RMSNorm, which \citet{kanavalau2026tapernorm} identify as the essential
        anchor of scale.
  \item $\tilde\gamma$ and all weight matrices remain trainable after the taper, so a tapered model can absorb a
        change of scale in its weights.
  \item RMSNorm makes each sub-layer's output invariant to the scale of the residual stream it reads, so weight
        decay can shrink the weights that set that scale without changing the function
        \citep{vanlaarhoven2017l2,zhang2019weightdecay}. This would explain the shrinkage of the RMS models'
        scale in both studies and the opposite movement in Taper-minus, whose internal layers depend on that
        scale once its gate is zero.
  \item With GPT-2's English-centred tokenizer, \twoCtrlShare\% of the Chinese labels are byte fragments. Much of
        the Chinese-branch forgetting may therefore come from the tied embedding and output layer, which the
        internal normalizers do not touch, rather than from the representations inside the network.
\end{itemize}
Testing these explanations would need targeted ablations, which neither study ran.

\paragraph{Specifying a cloud study in advance.} Study 2 shows that a study run on free cloud GPUs can be fixed
before it runs without a registry entry: hash the frozen protocol and code, timestamp the hash, embed the
manifest in every cloud job, and check every reused model state bit-exactly against its own records before it
trains. It also shows the weak point. The independent Bitcoin anchor arrived about \twoBlockAfterStart{} hours after the main run
started, so the earlier bound rests on the cloud provider's records. Waiting a few hours for the attestation
before starting the run, or filing a registry entry first, would remove that dependence at little cost.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Scale.} The models have \paramsRMS{} parameters, about \embedShare\% of them in embeddings, and see
        150M prefix tokens. Embedding drift may mediate or interact with the condition effect, and results may
        not transfer to larger models or longer training.
  \item \textbf{Breadth.} There are two domain pairs, one data sample per domain, one optimizer recipe, and one
        hardware and precision setting (FP16 on T4, not Study 1's originally planned BF16). The auxiliary-loss
        variant of TaperNorm (Taper-plus) was not run.
  \item \textbf{Statistics.} Seeds are few, and the realized seed-to-seed variation was far larger than Study 1's
        three seeds suggested and than Study 2's planning assumed. All intervals are descriptive $t$-intervals,
        conditional on one prepared corpus per domain. Every measurement uses development data; the reserved test
        sets of both studies remain sealed.
  \item \textbf{The selected domain.} The selection statistic is a switch-time proxy, and it did not find a domain
        with a larger switch-time gap than Python. Chinese text under GPT-2's tokenizer mixes a change of content
        with a change of the predicted token distribution, and it destroyed most of the models' web performance in
        both architectures. Another domain, or a tokenizer that covers Chinese, might behave differently.
  \item \textbf{The manipulation check.} Its in-training measure is taken after treatment, in models that had
        forgotten different amounts, so it cannot separate a premise that causes forgetting from a symptom of it.
        The measure taken before treatment, the switch-time gap, was not stronger for Chinese.
  \item \textbf{Reuse across sessions.} For seeds 101--103, Study 2's Chinese branches are compared with web
        branches trained in Study 1's session. The bit-exact lineage checks and the pinned software image bound
        the differences between sessions but cannot exclude all of them. The same seeds' switch states also served
        the domain selection.
  \item \textbf{Pre-specification.} Neither study was lodged with a registry before it ran. For Study 1, the
        earliest public record of the protocol and analysis code, a git commit, postdates both the start of the
        primary run and the appearance of seed 101's endpoint values in the run's live log
        (\cref{sec:prespec}); the evidence that its analysis was fixed in advance is the configuration hash logged
        before any outcome and the unchanged runner hash. For Study 2, the Bitcoin attestation postdates the
        Chinese endpoints of seeds 101--103, and the earlier bound rests on Kaggle's server-side records
        (\cref{sec:s2prespec}). Git commit dates are set by the author's machine and are not independent
        timestamps.
  \item \textbf{Thresholds.} The decision thresholds were set as investment thresholds without a power analysis,
        and Study 2's equivalence bound reuses the smaller of them. The one-sided readings and the
        leave-one-seed-out analysis were added after the results were known.
  \item \textbf{Data audit.} The near-duplicate search is approximate. Its analytic recall is high for pairs above
        the similarity threshold, but the random-pair audits are only sanity checks with negligible power,
        repository names are an incomplete proxy for forks, and near duplicates between the Chinese data and
        Study 1's training arrays were not searched.
  \item \textbf{Secondary and exploratory analyses.} Matched adaptation interpolates between evaluated
        checkpoints and describes curves; it does not evaluate an actual model. The trajectory windows, Study 1's
        scale analysis, its document bootstrap and the trajectory noise in Study 2 were examined after the results
        were known. The scale measures use the last 256 training windows of each domain, which the models may have
        seen, and they describe mean scales over tokens, whereas RMSNorm also normalizes variation between
        individual tokens. Attention-entropy and gain diagnostics were logged but are not analysed here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Two small studies, each specified before it ran, found no evidence that TaperNorm increases persistent forgetting
after a domain shift. For web to Python, the pre-specified decision was \emph{stop} in the pilot and again on
three fresh seeds, although the fresh seeds' contrasts were positive where the pilot's had been negative. For web
to Chinese, where a pre-specified in-training measure of the premise was about four times larger, mean $D_X$ was
\twoDmean{}~nats/token, just past the threshold for the opposite direction, with a 95\% interval that includes
zero; a post hoc one-sided bound rules out an excess of $+0.015$. Neither study shows equivalence, and
seed-to-seed variation was much larger than the pilot suggested. A
more decisive test would need larger models, many more seeds, a tokenizer suited to the new domain, and an
endpoint that averages several late evaluations.
