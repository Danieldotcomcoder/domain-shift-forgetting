\section{Introduction}

Normalization layers such as LayerNorm \citep{ba2016layer} and RMSNorm \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm} are a standard
part of Transformer language models. In the common pre-norm arrangement \citep{xiong2020layer}, every attention
and MLP sub-layer reads its input through a normalizer that rescales each token's activation vector by that
token's own statistics. A long line of work has asked whether normalization is necessary at all
\citep{zhang2019fixup,brock2021nfnet,he2024simplifying,zhu2025dyt}. TaperNorm \citep{kanavalau2026tapernorm} is a
recent proposal for Transformers; we follow version~1 of its arXiv preprint throughout. It acts exactly as RMSNorm
(or LayerNorm) during an initial gate warm-up, calibrates a fixed scaling branch during that warm-up, and then
cosine-decays a global gate until each internal normalizer becomes a sample-independent linear map. That map can
be folded into the adjacent linear projections at inference. \citet{kanavalau2026tapernorm} report that this
matches normalized baselines under identical setups, and they measure up to $1.22\times$ higher throughput in an
inference microbenchmark.

This study looks at a property that the original evaluation did not target: behaviour under a later shift in the
training distribution. A normalizer is invariant to the scale of its input. If continued training on a new
domain changes activation magnitudes, an RMSNorm layer absorbs part of that change automatically, whereas a
tapered layer applies a scale calibrated on the earlier distribution. One can conjecture that the tapered network
must then absorb the shift through weight updates, which might overwrite structure that supports the original
domain and so increase catastrophic forgetting
\citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic,french1999catastrophic,kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. This argument motivates the
hypothesis; the experiment tests the hypothesis, not the mechanism.

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} \textbf{H1}: after training switches from web text to Python code, internal TaperNorm
(without the auxiliary scale loss; final RMSNorm retained) increases the persistent deterioration of held-out web
cross-entropy, relative to continuing web training, compared with internal RMSNorm. \emph{Persistent} is defined
in \cref{sec:estimands}.

We ran a deliberately small, inexpensive pilot to decide whether H1 deserves a larger study. Its design
(\cref{sec:design}) pairs the two conditions on initialization and data order and branches each condition's
trained prefix into a web continuation and a Python continuation from an identical saved state. The design also
fixes a difference-in-differences endpoint and an ordered set of decision rules before any training. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item a paired, pre-specified design for measuring how an architectural choice affects forgetting under a
        domain shift, whose primary contrast decomposes exactly into branch-level forgetting, a control-branch
        term, per-document effects and per-token-class effects;
  \item the pilot's result. All validity checks pass and the pre-specified decision is \emph{stop: small observed
        effect}. Mean $D$ = \Dmean{}~nats/token, against a $+0.015$ bound for a meaningful excess;
  \item exploratory analyses, labelled as such, suggesting that the premise of H1 was only weakly present: the
        code-specific change in activation scale was small and similar in both architectures;
  \item code, frozen configuration, data hashes and all evaluation and training records, sufficient to recompute
        every number in this paper.
\end{itemize}
The study is small (\paramsRMS{} parameters, three seeds, one domain pair) and should be read as a screening
result, not as a general statement about TaperNorm (\cref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Normalization and its removal.} LayerNorm \citep{ba2016layer} and RMSNorm \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm}
normalize each token independently, and the pre-norm placement \citep{xiong2020layer} is now standard in
decoder-only language models. Fixup \citep{zhang2019fixup} and NFNets \citep{brock2021nfnet} train deep residual
networks without normalization through initialization, scaling and clipping choices. For Transformers,
\citet{he2024simplifying} study which standard components of the block can be removed, and
\citet{zhu2025dyt} replace normalization layers with an element-wise $\tanh$ map. TaperNorm
\citep{kanavalau2026tapernorm} instead removes internal normalization gradually during training. Its analysis
attributes the essential role of normalization to the \emph{output} normalizer, which anchors scale; this is why
our Taper-minus condition keeps the final RMSNorm.

\paragraph{Normalization, weight decay and effective learning rate.} When a normalizer makes a network's output
invariant to the scale of some weights, weight decay no longer regularizes those weights in the usual sense;
it shrinks them and thereby raises their effective learning rate \citep{vanlaarhoven2017l2,zhang2019weightdecay}.
\citet{lyle2024normalization} connect normalization and effective learning rates to the loss of plasticity under
nonstationary training. These results bear on our exploratory scale analysis (\cref{sec:exploratory}).

\paragraph{Forgetting under continued training.} Catastrophic forgetting
\citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic,french1999catastrophic} is the loss of earlier competence when a network is
trained on new data, and it has motivated methods such as elastic weight consolidation
\citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. The training regime, including learning-rate decay, affects how much a network
forgets \citep{mirzadeh2020training}; larger pre-trained models forget less \citep{ramasesh2022scale}; and the
choice of normalization layer matters in online continual learning \citep{pham2022continual}. In language
modelling, continued pre-training on a new domain improves in-domain performance \citep{gururangan2020dont}, but
it can degrade performance on the original distribution; \citet{gupta2023rewarm} and \citet{ibrahim2024simple}
study how learning-rate re-warming, re-decaying and replay trade adaptation against this forgetting. Our design
deliberately uses no replay, re-warming or other mitigation. It isolates how much forgetting the normalization
choice itself causes, relative to a matched control branch.

\paragraph{Pre-specification.} Fixing hypotheses, endpoints and analysis rules before seeing outcomes protects
against flexible analysis \citep{nosek2018preregistration}. \Cref{sec:prespec} states exactly what was fixed, and
when, including one disclosure about the timing of the public record.

\section{Pre-specified design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Notation and estimands}
\label{sec:estimands}

We write $u$ for the global optimizer update ($u = 1,\dots,15{,}260$) and $s = u - 9{,}156$ for the continuation
update within a branch. Two different events fall on the number 6{,}104: the TaperNorm gate reaches zero at global
update $u = 6{,}104$, and the primary endpoint is continuation update $s = 6{,}104$ (global update $u = 15{,}260$).
They coincide because the protocol ends the taper at 40\% of the 15{,}260-update trajectory, which equals the
continuation length. We always state whether a number is a $u$ or an $s$.

Let $L(m, b, s)$ be the token-weighted cross-entropy on the held-out web development set (nats per supervised
token, 2{,}097{,}152 labels), for normalization condition $m \in \{\mathrm{R}, \mathrm{T}\}$ (RMS, Taper-minus),
after $s$ continuation updates on branch $b \in \{\text{web}, \text{code}\}$. Let $L(m,\text{prefix})$ be the
same quantity at the switch. The primary endpoint is
\begin{equation}
  D(s) = \big[L(\mathrm{T},\text{code},s) - L(\mathrm{T},\text{web},s)\big] - \big[L(\mathrm{R},\text{code},s) - L(\mathrm{R},\text{web},s)\big],
  \label{eq:d}
\end{equation}
evaluated at $s = 6{,}104$. Positive $D$ means that, relative to its own web-continuation control, the Taper-minus
model loses more web performance under the Python shift than the RMS model does. Within each condition the
code-minus-web difference cancels everything the two branches share, including the condition-specific quality of
the prefix and generic continued-training drift.

\paragraph{Persistent.} We call a deterioration \emph{persistent} if it is present at the fixed endpoint
$s = 6{,}104$, after 100M continuation tokens and at the end of the learning-rate schedule. A difference visible
only early in continuation ($s \le 1{,}000$) is \emph{transient}, which the decision rules treat separately
(\cref{tab:rules}, rule~5).

We also report the actual forgetting $F(m,b) = L(m,b,6104) - L(m,\text{prefix})$ and the change in the
between-condition gap during web continuation,
$G_{\text{web}} = [L(\mathrm{T},\text{prefix}) - L(\mathrm{R},\text{prefix})] -
[L(\mathrm{T},\text{web},6104) - L(\mathrm{R},\text{web},6104)]$. These satisfy the identity
\begin{equation}
  D = \underbrace{F(\mathrm{T},\text{code}) - F(\mathrm{R},\text{code})}_{Q} + G_{\text{web}},
\end{equation}
so $Q = D - G_{\text{web}}$ is the direct difference in Python-branch forgetting. The identity is checked
numerically in the analysis code. Because CE is token-weighted, $D$ decomposes exactly into per-document terms
$D_i$ (weighted by each document's scored-label count) and into four disjoint GPT-2 token classes: whitespace
(W), text containing an alphanumeric character (A), other punctuation and symbols (P), and special, control or
invalid-fragment tokens (X). A fifth, overlapping flag R marks rare tokens: those that occur fewer than 100 times
in that seed's prefix training stream.

\subsection{Models and conditions}

All models are pre-norm decoder-only Transformers adapted from nanoGPT \citep{karpathy2022nanogpt}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item 6 layers, width 256, 4 heads of size 64, and an exact-GELU MLP of width 1{,}024;
  \item learned absolute positions, context 512, no biases and no dropout;
  \item GPT-2 token embeddings \citep{radford2019gpt2}, tied to the output layer (vocabulary 50{,}257).
\end{itemize}
The RMS model has \paramsRMS{} parameters; Taper-minus adds 3{,}072 for its second gain vectors. About
\embedShare\% of all parameters are in the token-embedding table. Both conditions keep the final RMSNorm.

\paragraph{Initialization.} All linear and embedding weights (token and position) are drawn from
$\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$. The two residual output projections of each block (the attention output projection and
the MLP down-projection) use $\mathcal{N}\big(0, (0.02/\sqrt{12})^2\big)$, where $12 = 2 \times 6$ layers. All gains
start at~1.

\paragraph{RMS.} All 12 internal normalizers are RMSNorm with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$.

\paragraph{Taper-minus.} All 12 internal normalizers are TaperNorm, following the operator and calibration of
\citet{kanavalau2026tapernorm} (their Section~3 and Appendix~C), without their auxiliary scale loss. For an input
$h \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at global update $u$, with gains $\gamma$ and $\tilde\gamma$,
\begin{equation}
  r(h) = \sqrt{\lVert h\rVert_2^2/d + 10^{-6}}, \qquad
  y = g(u)\, \frac{h}{r(h)} \odot \gamma + \big(1 - g(u)\big)\, c\, h \odot \tilde\gamma .
\end{equation}
The scalar $c$ of each site is calibrated during updates $u = 1$--$763$ as the ratio of bias-corrected
exponential moving averages (one EMA step per optimizer update, new-sample weight $0.01$) of
$\lVert h\odot\gamma\rVert^2 / r(h)$ and $\lVert h\odot\gamma\rVert^2$. These are collected from the pre-step
forward passes of every microbatch. After update $u = 763$, $c$ is frozen and $\tilde\gamma$ is initialized from
$\gamma$; $\tilde\gamma$ first receives an optimizer step at $u = 764$ and stays trainable. The gate is $g(u) = 1$
for $u \le 763$, $g(u) = \tfrac12[1 + \cos(\pi (u - 763)/5341)]$ for $763 < u < 6104$, and $g(u) = 0$ for
$u \ge 6104$. At $g = 0$ no per-token statistic is computed and $\gamma$ receives no gradient. Until $u = 763$ the
two conditions are mathematically identical. The gate timing, GELU, learned positions, weight decay,
learning-rate schedule and domain branching are choices of this study, so this is an adaptation of TaperNorm, not
a replication of its experiments.

\paragraph{Optimization.} The optimizer is AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with $\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$ and
$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. All two-dimensional parameters, embeddings included, get weight decay $0.1$; gain vectors
get none. Gradients are clipped once per update to global norm $1.0$, after accumulation. Each update uses
$32 \times 512 = 16{,}384$ supervised tokens. The learning rate rises linearly to $6\times10^{-4}$ over the first
305 updates and then follows a cosine decay to $6\times10^{-5}$ at $u = 15{,}260$. The branches continue this
global clock, with no learning-rate restart, optimizer reset, replay or regularization toward the prefix
(\cref{fig:design}).

\paragraph{Pairing.} Seeds 101, 102 and 103 are paired across conditions. A canonical RMS initialization is
generated from the seed and explicitly copied into the Taper-minus model; $\tilde\gamma$ starts at~1 and is
inactive until $u = 764$. Both conditions read the same pre-generated window order. Each run consists of a prefix
of 9{,}156 updates (150{,}011{,}904 tokens) followed by two branches of 6{,}104 updates each (100{,}007{,}936
tokens). The web branch reads further unseen web windows and the Python branch reads Python windows; no window
repeats within a trajectory. The primary allocation is 2.10B supervised tokens.

\paragraph{Precision.} Training ran in FP16 autocast with dynamic loss scaling \citep{micikevicius2018mixed}.
Master weights, optimizer moments, normalization and calibration statistics, and the loss were kept in FP32.
A step whose gradients were nonfinite was to be retried on the same batch at a halved loss scale, so no update
is ever skipped. This replaced the protocol's original BF16 plan, because the available hardware (NVIDIA T4)
lacks efficient BF16 support (\cref{tab:amendments}).

\subsection{Data}

\paragraph{Sources and splits.} Web text comes from C4 English \citep{raffel2020t5}. Training uses the official
training split, and the official validation split is hashed 50/50 into development and reserved-test sets. Code
comes from the Python subset of The Stack (deduplicated) \citep{kocetkov2022stack}. The dataset revisions are
\texttt{\cfourRev} (C4) and \texttt{\stackRev} (The Stack). Python files are grouped into connected components
over their repository-name aliases (\repoGroups{} groups). Each group is assigned to a split by the value of
$\mathrm{sha256}(\text{group}) \bmod 100$: 0--89 train, 90--94 development and 95--99 test. Empty documents, and
Python files without a usable repository name, were dropped.

\paragraph{Deduplication.} Documents are deduplicated exactly by content hash across all domains and splits. An
approximate near-duplicate search uses MinHash \citep{broder1997resemblance} over GPT-2 token 5-grams (128
permutations, LSH with 32 bands of 4 rows), confirmed by exact Jaccard similarity $\ge 0.85$. When duplicates
straddle splits, the copy in the higher-priority split is kept (test over development over training). This
removed \exactRemoved{} exact and \nearRemoved{} near duplicates. Documents shorter than five tokens have no
5-gram and are covered only by exact deduplication (\shortDocs{} documents).

\paragraph{Recall of the near-duplicate search.} With 32 bands of 4 rows, a pair with Jaccard similarity $J$
becomes an LSH candidate with probability $1 - (1 - J^4)^{32}$. A pair at $J = 0.85$ is therefore missed with
probability \lshMiss, and a pair at $J = 0.70$ is still found with probability \lshRecallSeventy. As an empirical
check, a deterministic random sample of \auditPairs{} cross-split document pairs was compared by exact Jaccard,
and none reached 0.85. That sampled audit has low power to detect rare leakage (\cref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Packing and quotas.} Text is tokenized with GPT-2 BPE, with one EOS appended per document, and packed
into 513-token windows at stride 512 with no padding. Windows are packed across document boundaries without an
attention mask: a window can hold the end of one document, an EOS token and the start of the next, and attention
can cross the EOS. Positions restart at 0 in every window. Each label is attributed to its own document (a label
after an EOS belongs to the new document), so per-document sums reconstruct the corpus CE exactly. Quotas are
\webTrainTokens{} web and \pyTrainTokens{} Python training tokens, and 2{,}097{,}152 development labels (4{,}096
windows) per domain. The \emph{quick} development set is the first 262{,}144 of those labels. Reserved-test sets
of 8{,}388{,}608 labels per domain were sealed and never used. The non-whitespace code CE used to measure
adaptation excludes class W only.

\subsection{Evaluation schedule and decision rules}

Full development evaluations ran at 13 prefix points and at 25 continuation points per branch. Quick development
evaluations ran at the switch and at 80 continuation points per branch, dense early on (\cref{app:schedule}).
Every full evaluation stores per-document and per-class sufficient statistics.

The secondary \emph{matched-adaptation} analysis is fixed in advance. It fixes targets at the RMS Python branch's
quick-dev code CE at $s \in \{1525, 3050, 6104\}$. It finds the first downward crossing of each target by
Taper-minus within an observed bracket of at most 100 updates (no extrapolation) and interpolates Taper-minus's
web CE at that crossing. It then compares the two models' web forgetting relative to their own $s = 0$.

The decision rules in \cref{tab:rules} are applied in order once all three seed groups are complete. They are
screening criteria, not significance tests. With three seeds, the reported 95\% interval
$\bar D \pm 4.303\, s_D/\sqrt{3}$ is descriptive.

\paragraph{Choice of thresholds.} The protocol fixed the thresholds of 0.015 and 0.03~nats/token in advance as
investment thresholds for a screening pilot. It records no power analysis or formal derivation for them, and we
do not supply one after the fact. For orientation, a cross-entropy difference of 0.015 or 0.03~nats/token
corresponds to a 1.5\% or 3.0\% change in perplexity ($e^{0.015} \approx 1.015$, $e^{0.03} \approx 1.030$).
A mean between the two thresholds falls into the inconclusive category rather than into either decision.

\subsection{Pre-specification, timeline and amendments}
\label{sec:prespec}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The hypothesis, design, estimands, evaluation schedule and decision thresholds come from a
written protocol (``protocol v3''), drafted on 12 September 2026, before the corpus was prepared or any model was
trained on it. The protocol text is included in the project repository. It was not lodged with an external
registry, which is a limitation (\cref{sec:limitations}). The amendments in \cref{tab:amendments} were made before
any primary-run update; none of them changes the model, data, token budgets, seeds, endpoint or thresholds.

\paragraph{Timeline of the primary run (3 October 2026, UTC).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \sessionStartTime: the run starts.
  \item \logConfigTime: the run log records the frozen configuration hash (\texttt{\configSha\ldots}). The
        hashed configuration contains the decision thresholds, and the hash can be recomputed from the released
        \texttt{config.json}.
  \item \seedOneFirst--\seedOneLast: seed 101's four endpoint cross-entropies (both conditions, both branches)
        appear in the run's live log. Seed 101's $D$ could have been computed from them.
  \item \commitTime: commit \texttt{3ee1dc4} is made, containing the protocol text and the runner script.
  \item \sessionEndTime: the run ends, and the same process applies the decision rules and writes the decision
        report.
\end{itemize}
Because the commit came after seed 101's endpoint values had been printed, the commit on its own does not
establish pre-specification, and we disclose that here and in \cref{sec:limitations}. The evidence that the
analysis was fixed in advance is different. The thresholds used by the run were bound into the configuration
hash logged at \logConfigTime, before any outcome existed. The runner script, whose decision thresholds appear on
line~\thresholdLine, has the same SHA-256 (\texttt{\runnerSha\ldots}) in every commit of the repository history,
and that hash matches the one recorded by the run itself. The decision report was produced by that same process.

\paragraph{Engineering runs.} Before the primary run, engineering runs measured throughput and verified
checkpoint resume. All of them were discarded:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item local development runs on stand-in corpora (WikiText-103 and CodeSearchNet functions), which trained RMS
        models only;
  \item preflights of an earlier execution pipeline under engineering seed 997, which timed each training phase by
        repositioning the update clock rather than by training a prefix to the switch;
  \item a smoke test of about 20 minutes on the final runner, which repeated the first 2{,}197 (Taper-minus) and
        2{,}336 (RMS) prefix updates of seed 101.
\end{itemize}
None of them produced a Taper-minus-versus-RMS continuation contrast on the C4 and Stack data, so no value of
$D$ was computed before the primary run.

\section{Execution and implementation checks}
\label{sec:execution}

The primary run took one \sessionHours-hour session on a cloud machine with two NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs (PyTorch
\torchVersion, CUDA \cudaVersion), with RMS on one GPU and Taper-minus on the other. All six condition--seed runs
completed all 21{,}364 updates. There were no nonfinite losses, gradients or weights; the loss scale grew to
\maxLossScale{} and never needed a retry (\overflowRetries{} retries in total). GPU kernels used for the backward
pass are nondeterministic, so results are reproducible to within numerical noise, not bitwise.

\paragraph{Gradient clipping.} Clipping at global norm 1.0 is part of the same optimization recipe for both

\paragraph{Implementation checks.} The training code is a single script that transcribes a separately tested
reference implementation of the model, optimizer step and analysis. Before the run, it was checked against that
reference in seven ways:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item identical learning-rate and gate values at all 15{,}260 updates;
  \item identical paired initial tensors;
  \item FP32 training updates during the calibration phase that match the reference update to a relative
        tolerance of $2\times10^{-5}$;
  \item bit-identical FP32 model outputs at updates spanning every TaperNorm gate state;
  \item a vectorized evaluator that reproduces the reference evaluator's CE and class sums on the real
        development data, to a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$;
  \item identical decision categories on 3{,}000 randomly generated evidence sets;
  \item checkpoint resume whose deviation from an uninterrupted run is of the same order as run-to-run GPU
        nondeterminism.
\end{enumerate}
The primary contrasts in \cref{sec:results} were also recomputed independently from the raw evaluation records,
and they agree with the frozen analysis output.

The other pre-specified quantities point the same way as $D$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item $G_{\text{web}}$ is close to zero (mean \Gmean), so the difference sits in the Python branch itself: the

  \item The matched-adaptation contrast at $s = \matchedTarget$ averages \matchedMean{}, negative in every seed.

\earlyMax{} (at $s = \earlyMaxAt$) within the first 1{,}000 updates, against a threshold of 0.05.

\subsection{Exploratory analyses (not pre-specified)}
\label{sec:exploratory}

The analyses in this subsection were chosen after the results were known. They are descriptive, and none of
them enters the decision.

\paragraph{Activation scales at the normalizer inputs.} The run logged forward-only probes of the activation
$h$ entering each of the 12 internal normalizers, on the last 256 training windows of each domain (131{,}072
positions per domain), at the switch and at the end of each branch. For each site we take the scale to be the
root mean square of $\lVert h\rVert$ over the probe positions, compute log-ratios of scales, average them over
the 12 sites and the 3 seeds, and report the result as a percentage. \Cref{tab:scales} gives three quantities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The code-vs-web gap at the switch.} Code activations differed in scale from web activations by
        \scaleGapR\% (RMS) and \scaleGapT\% (Taper-minus), in absolute value per site.
  \item \textbf{The change in the web-probe scale during each branch.} In the RMS model, the scale changed by
        about \scaleWebAbsR\% per site in the web branch and \scalePyAbsR\% in the Python branch, mostly by
        shrinking (signed means $\scaleWebSignedR\%$ and $\scalePySignedR\%$). In Taper-minus it changed by only
        \scaleWebAbsT\% and \scalePyAbsT\% (signed means $\scaleWebSignedT\%$ and $\scalePySignedT\%$).
  \item \textbf{The code-specific part of that change} (Python branch minus web branch): \scaleSpecificR\%
        (RMS) and \scaleSpecificT\% (Taper-minus) per site.
\end{itemize}
The largest scale change, the shrinkage in the RMS model, happened equally in both branches, so it was not caused
by the code data. The code-specific change was small and the same size in both architectures.

\paragraph{Document bootstrap.} To see how much of the uncertainty in $D$ comes from the finite development set,
we resampled the \nDocsWeb{} web development documents with replacement (\bootB{} replicates; the same resampled
documents for all arms and seeds) and recomputed the endpoint $D$. The 95\% percentile interval for mean $D$ is
$[\bootLo, \bootHi]$, with a bootstrap standard error of \bootSE; per-seed standard errors are
\bootSeedSElo--\bootSeedSEhi{} (\cref{app:extra}). This interval treats the trained models as fixed, so it
measures only evaluation-sample noise. The between-seed SD of $D$ (\Dsd) is about \bootRatio{} times larger than
that standard error. Uncertainty about $D$ is therefore dominated by training variation across seeds, and the
bootstrap interval must not be read as uncertainty about the effect itself.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the result shows.} In this setting, replacing internal RMSNorm with TaperNorm did not increase
persistent forgetting of web text after a switch to Python. The pre-specified screen for a meaningful excess
($\bar D \ge 0.015$) was not met, and every seed's endpoint $D$ was below zero. Under the protocol's rules this
direction is stopped. No seeds were added, no endpoint was changed and no follow-up was launched.

\paragraph{What it does not show.} \emph{Stop} is not evidence of equivalence \citep{lakens2017equivalence}, and
the negative endpoint is not evidence that TaperNorm protects against forgetting. That effect would have needed
$\bar D \le -0.03$, which was not reached. Apart from a brief early dip, the negative values appeared in the
final, low-learning-rate phase, and the training regime, including learning-rate decay, is known to affect
forgetting
\citep{mirzadeh2020training}. Whether TaperNorm interacts with the end of the schedule is an open question that
this pilot was not designed to answer. Seed-to-seed variation in neural-network training can be large
\citep{dodge2020finetuning}, and three seeds estimate it poorly. The descriptive interval is conditional on one
prepared corpus sample and one training recipe; it is not a population bound.

\paragraph{Why the feared mechanism may have had little to act on.} H1 rests on a story: training on code changes
activation magnitudes, RMSNorm absorbs the change, and a fixed TaperNorm scale cannot. The exploratory scale
analysis (\cref{sec:exploratory}) suggests that the starting condition of this story was only weakly present
in our models:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item At the switch, code and web activations differed in scale by only \scaleGapR--\scaleGapT\% on average.
  \item The largest scale change during continuation, the shrinkage in the RMS model, occurred equally when
        training simply continued on web text, so it was not a response to code.
  \item The code-specific scale change was about \scaleSpecificR\% per site in both architectures.
\end{itemize}
A plausible reason for the RMS shrinkage is that RMSNorm makes each sub-layer's output invariant to the scale of
the residual stream it reads, so weight decay can shrink the weights that set that scale without changing the
function \citep{vanlaarhoven2017l2,zhang2019weightdecay}. Once its gate is zero, Taper-minus's internal layers
depend on that scale, which would oppose the same shrinkage. We did not test this explanation. Two further
features of the setting may also matter: Taper-minus keeps the final RMSNorm, which \citet{kanavalau2026tapernorm}
identify as the essential scale anchor, and $\tilde\gamma$ and all weight matrices remain trainable after the
taper.

These observations carry four caveats. They are exploratory, not pre-specified. The probes use the last 256
training windows of each domain, which the models may have seen during training. They describe mean scales over
tokens, whereas RMSNorm also normalizes variation between individual tokens. And they are descriptive, not
causal: they show that the premise was weak, not that this is why there was no effect.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Scale.} The models have \paramsRMS{} parameters, about \embedShare\% of them in embeddings, and see
        150M prefix tokens. Embedding drift may mediate or interact with the condition effect, and results may
        not transfer to larger models or longer training.
  \item \textbf{Breadth.} There is one domain pair (web to Python), one data sample, one optimizer recipe and one
        hardware and precision setting (FP16 on T4, not the protocol's original BF16). The auxiliary-loss variant
        of TaperNorm (Taper-plus) was not run.
  \item \textbf{Statistics.} Three paired seeds share one prepared corpus, so uncertainty is conditional on that
        corpus, and the interval in \cref{tab:primary} is descriptive. All measurements use development data; the
        reserved test sets remain sealed for any confirmatory study.
  \item \textbf{Pre-specification.} The protocol was kept privately and not registered externally. The earliest
        public record of the protocol and analysis code, a git commit, postdates the start of the primary run,
        and it also postdates the appearance of seed 101's endpoint values in the run's live log
        (\cref{sec:prespec}). Git commit dates are set by the author's machine and are not independent
        timestamps. The evidence that the analysis was fixed in advance is the configuration hash logged before
        any outcome and the unchanged runner hash, not the commit date.
  \item \textbf{Thresholds.} The decision thresholds were set as investment thresholds without a power analysis.
  \item \textbf{Data audit.} The near-duplicate search is approximate. Its analytic recall is high for pairs above
        the similarity threshold, but the random audit of \auditPairs{} cross-split document pairs has low power
        to detect rare leakage, and repository names are an incomplete proxy for forks.
  \item \textbf{Secondary and exploratory analyses.} Matched adaptation interpolates between evaluated
        checkpoints and describes curves; it does not evaluate an actual model. The trajectory windows, the scale
        analysis and the document bootstrap were chosen after the results were known. Attention-entropy and gain
        diagnostics were logged but are not analysed here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A small pilot, specified before training, found no evidence that TaperNorm increases persistent forgetting after
a web-to-code domain shift: mean $D$ = \Dmean{}~nats/token, against a pre-specified $+0.015$ bound. After a
brief early dip, the difference stayed near zero until the final, low-learning-rate phase, when it turned
negative; it was small, so we draw no conclusion about protection. Exploratory analyses suggest that the mechanism behind the concern had little to act on: the
code-specific change in activation scale was small and similar in both architectures. Testing H1 more broadly
would need a new pre-specified study with larger models, more seeds and more than one domain shift, ideally one
that measures the scale premise directly. On its own, this pilot does not justify that study.
